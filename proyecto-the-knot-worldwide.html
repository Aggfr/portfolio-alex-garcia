Freemium to Premium

We believe that limiting the feature set available in the Lite vendor experience will increase conversion from free to paid vendors, because it increases Lite FOMO and the need to convert to Paid.

Client The Knot Worldwide · 2024–2025
Responsibility Strategy Design · UX Design · Research · Design Thinking · Lead Strategy
Area Growth & Monetization
Partners Cross-functional stakeholders across Product, Design and Research

Lite and paid vendors moved through almost the same product. They saw the same dashboard, the same core tools, and in most cases the same features, with nothing in the experience signaling that a paid plan unlocked more. Without that signal, there was no natural pressure pushing vendors toward Premium, and every team touching monetization was compensating for it differently: Premium prompts and upsell flows had been built feature by feature, by different teams, at different times, with no shared logic behind when or how to show them.

Before designing anything, I needed to understand who we were actually limiting the experience for. Working with research, we segmented vendors into two groups: price-sensitive vendors, who compare plans line by line before committing to anything, and risk-averse vendors, who need to see Premium actually working for their business before they'll pay for it. Those two groups don't respond to the same nudge, so I mapped the vendor journey stage by stage to find where each group's hesitation showed up, and where a psychological or cognitive conversion principle would land instead of feeling like a generic upsell. From there, I ran a fishbone exercise with stakeholders across Product, Sales and Support to break "increase free-to-paid conversion" down into objectives each department could actually act on, since a goal that abstract tends to get reinterpreted differently by every team that touches it. In parallel, a benchmark of how comparable products frame their own free-to-paid limitations became the evidence base for what good restraint looks like versus what tips into a dark pattern.

The resulting strategy kept Lite vendors on basic storefront and admin functionality, while making exactly what they'd gain from upgrading hard to miss. Every limitation had to hold up against five principles before it shipped: transparency, simplicity, honesty, ethical framing and education, which in practice meant ruling out forced actions, nagging reminders, hidden costs and fake social proof, the kind of dark patterns that convert in the short term and erode trust in the long term. Rather than rolling this out everywhere at once, we shipped it first on two features we could learn from quickly, Reviews and Social Media, replacing a hard block on each with an inline banner that flagged the feature as Premium in context, followed by a value-focused modal that showed vendors what they'd gain instead of just telling them what they couldn't do.

Why it mattered

A platform that looks the same regardless of plan has no built-in reason for anyone to upgrade, and TKWW was compensating for that gap with scattered, inconsistent prompts that different teams had built independently of each other. That meant wasted effort rebuilding the same kind of nudge over and over, and a free-to-paid transition that depended on whichever team touched it last, rather than a deliberate, company-wide strategy.

Key decision

A hard block was the obvious option, and also the one most likely to feel punitive instead of persuasive: it tells a vendor what they can't do without showing them why they'd want to. Instead, we designed a two-layer pattern: an inline banner that marks a feature as Premium right where the vendor encounters it, in context, followed by a modal that shows the value behind a blurred preview before pointing to a clear upgrade CTA. The same five principles applied everywhere this pattern replaced a block, so the limitation felt like a consistent, intentional part of the product rather than a wall vendors ran into at random.

Outcome

The pattern shipped first on two features: the old “Respond” action on Reviews, and the previously blocked Social Media form, both replaced with Premium-gated versions that explain the value before asking vendors to upgrade. Starting with two well-scoped features instead of a platform-wide rollout let us validate that the banner-plus-modal pattern actually worked in production and gather real vendor feedback before investing further. That validated pattern is now the template the team reaches for on every new Premium limitation, which is where the real leverage of this project sits: not in a single feature, but in a reusable, principle-based system for the free-to-paid transition going forward.

Sign up & Checkout

This project introduces a self-checkout flow to simplify onboarding for Starter vendors. By automating manual steps, we improve the vendor experience.

Client The Knot Worldwide · 2024
Responsibility Research · UX Design · Data Driven · Visual Design
Area Growth & Onboarding
Partners Internal Sales & Support teams

Create a full self-checkout for Starter vendors, reducing manual onboarding steps while improving the vendor experience and registration volume.

-50% Reduced internal support load
+35% Boosted paid Starter unit conversion
+26% Increased vendor registration rate

Onboarding a new vendor onto TKWW was a largely manual process. A vendor would submit a lead, and from there an internal team had to create their account and build out their storefront before they could start advertising. That handoff added days to something that should have taken minutes, and every extra day was a vendor who could lose interest, sign with a competitor, or simply drop out. Sales and support were repeating the same setup work for every sign up, a workload about to compound with the Starter Tier pilot launching in 2024 H2.

To find out where the friction actually lived, I looked at drop-off across the registration funnel and reviewed onboarding support tickets with the support team. Both pointed to the same bottleneck: vendors weren't struggling with the sign up form itself, they were stalling at the handoff to an internal team. That gave us a clear hypothesis: remove the manual handoff and let vendors complete checkout on their own, and paid unit volume would go up while time to paid subscription would go down. From there, I ran several rounds of wireframes and clickable prototypes, testing for the simplest self-checkout that still felt trustworthy: visible progress indicators so vendors knew where they stood, a payment step that didn't feel riskier than it needed to, and a confirmation screen so no one was left wondering if the purchase went through.

This work sat under TKWW's Future Of Monetization objective, which targeted 2,300 incremental paid units (1,200 Starter and 1,100 Core) by year end. Self-checkout was one of the main levers for the Starter half of that goal: letting vendors activate their own account in minutes, instead of waiting on an internal team, removed the biggest bottleneck at the top of that funnel.

Why it mattered

Every manual sign up was a fixed cost in sales and support time that didn't scale, and that cost was about to multiply with the Starter Tier pilot ramping up in 2024 H2. Without a self-service path, growth in Starter adoption would have meant growth in support backlog, not growth in paid vendors.

Key decision

Rather than trying to make the manual process faster, we replaced it: a fully self-service checkout with clear progress indicators, simplified payment collection, legal terms acceptance and a confirmation step, so vendors could complete the whole flow on their own, with no sales or support involvement required.

Outcome

Once live, self-checkout cut internal support load tied to onboarding by 50%, lifted paid Starter unit conversion by 35%, and increased vendor registration rate by 26%, moving the Starter tier meaningfully closer to its Future Of Monetization target without adding headcount.

What scaled

The importance of reviews

The project focused on increasing review volume by simplifying how vendors share review links, reducing friction and eliminating complex navigation. This was expected to encourage more active review collection.

Client The Knot Worldwide · 2024
Responsibility Research · UX Design · Data Driven · Visual Design
Area Growth & Retention
Partners Add partners here

Reduce the time vendors spend collecting reviews by letting them share the review link directly from WeddingPro, instead of loading multiple pages across separate portals.

-50% Time to request a review
+35% Increase in reviews collected
+24% Improved conversion and engagement

TKWW vendors had to repeat the same actions twice to request reviews on each vendor portal, loading multiple pages with very limited sharing options for the reviews link. The goal was to reduce the time spent collecting reviews by letting vendors share the reviews link directly from WeddingPro.

Reviews are critical to success: 9 in 10 couples read reviews, and each new review has an incremental impact on performance — going from 0–5 reviews lifts performance ~17% on average, and 0–10 reviews lifts it ~28%. Vendors with 5+ reviews convert ~23% better on average than those with fewer, get 2.2x the storefront views, and 1.9x the leads of vendors with no reviews at all.

We designed a new page with quick, clear shortcuts to the main review-collection actions, prioritizing the action vendors preferred most — Copy link — through a stronger CTA and a more prominent position. With many actions competing for space, we kept the design visually simple and easy to scan to reduce cognitive load.

Why it mattered

Reviews directly move the metrics that matter most: conversion, storefront views and leads. Making them harder to collect than necessary was leaving that performance upside on the table.

Key decision

Prioritize the single action vendors reach for most — Copy link — with a stronger CTA and top placement, and keep the rest of the page visually simple so it stays easy to scan despite the number of available actions.

Outcome

Time to request a review dropped by 50%, the volume of reviews collected rose by 35%, and conversion and engagement improved by 24%.

Dashboard update

This project used the Jobs-to-be-Done framework to enhance the vendor experience by guiding them through their planning journey. The goal was to drive higher conversion rates to ULS.

Client The Knot Worldwide · 2024
Responsibility Research · UX Design · Data Driven · Visual Design · Design Thinking
Area Growth & Conversion
Partners Add partners here

Increase ULS conversion by focusing the vendor dashboard on what each user actually needs next, instead of showing the same generic modules to everyone.

+42% Increased ULS conversion rate
+38% Improved dashboard engagement

The goal of this project was to increase ULS by focusing user attention and prioritizing the information shown above the default view on the vendor dashboard. We believed that applying the Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) framework would increase conversion from dashboard users to ULS by offering them a path to follow through their planning journey.

The JTBD approach removes the focus from the product itself and places it on the customer and their needs. Applying it to the dashboard meant addressing three problems at once: personalization — the experience was nearly identical for every user regardless of which jobs they had completed; priorities & hierarchy — the dashboard offered every product with no clear path to follow; and positioning & interconnection — products were shown separately instead of connected to the user’s stage in their wedding planning journey.

The redesigned dashboard replaces the generic, one-size-fits-all modules with content ordered around the vendor’s actual progress — surfacing the next relevant action (guest list, budget, venue, vendor team) instead of a static list of every available product.

Why it mattered

Every user saw the same dashboard no matter what they had already completed, with no clear hierarchy or path guiding them toward the next step in their wedding planning.

Key decision

Apply the JTBD framework to personalize, prioritize and interconnect dashboard modules around each vendor’s planning journey, instead of listing every product with equal weight.

Outcome

ULS conversion rate increased by 42% and dashboard engagement improved by 38%.
