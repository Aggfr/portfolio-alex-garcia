Adding travel activities to our catalogue

Hellotickets integrated Viator's API to add trip experiences to its catalogue for the first time, which meant redesigning the site's information architecture around a product type it had never sold before.

Client Hellotickets
Responsibility Strategy Design · UX Design · Research · Design Thinking
Area Catalogue Expansion & Growth
Partners CEO · CTO · Marketing

Until recently, Hellotickets' business was built entirely on ticket sales for a large number of events, mainly across Europe and the US. To evolve the product and bring trip experiences into the catalogue for the first time, the company licensed Viator's API, gaining access to its entire product offering and the ability to start expanding the business beyond tickets. Making that integration real meant more than plugging in a feed: it required a major redesign of the website's information architecture to make room for a product type it had never carried before.

The hypothesis was that integrating Viator's API and redesigning the information architecture would significantly increase revenue and user engagement by expanding the catalogue to include trip experiences alongside event ticket sales. Since this was the first time the team was working in the travel experience market, we spent two months on research before touching the design, running 15 interviews with users from around the world to better understand why they chose a particular product or service, build real empathy for their experience, surface their pain points and information gaps, and understand their purchase behavior.

That research surfaced a consistent pattern: the market was crowded and standing out mattered, users carried real doubts about the activities they were considering, and they compared several options before buying, weighing price, free cancellation and language above everything else. They didn't want to register just to make a purchase, they ran the full funnel through to checkout just to see the final price and commissions, and they were willing to pay more on a site that gave them confidence. Those insights shaped a comprehensive redesign of the information architecture built around Viator's catalogue of trip experiences, aimed at a seamless experience that drives both engagement and conversion.

Why it mattered

Trip experiences were a product type Hellotickets had never sold before, and the existing information architecture had nowhere to put them. Without that redesign, the investment in Viator's API would have stayed a backend connection vendors couldn't discover or buy through, never turning into either revenue or engagement.

Key decision

Rather than bolting trip experiences onto the existing, ticket-focused structure, the information architecture was rebuilt around both product types from research, not assumption: two months and 15 interviews across markets shaped which paths and comparison points, price, free cancellation and language, the new structure had to support.

Outcome

What scaled

The category, subcategory and product structure built to carry trip experiences became the template for bringing any future product type into the catalogue, instead of a one-off exception bolted onto the existing site. The two months of upfront research before any design work also set the standard the team kept using: validate the paths and comparison points with real users first, then build the structure to support them.

Redesign of the detail page

Hellotickets' trip-experience detail page had grown dense and hard to scan, so the information architecture and visual hierarchy were rebuilt around what research showed users actually needed to decide.

Client Hellotickets
Responsibility Research · UX Design · Data Driven · Visual Design
Area Conversion & Growth
Partners CEO · CTO · Marketing

Every structural change on the page traced back to a specific, measured user behavior rather than a design assumption.

+32% Increased product page conversion rate
-28% Reduced bounce rate on key sections
+25% Increased check-availability to purchase conversion

As Hellotickets expanded into trip experiences, the detail page became the single most important page in the purchase decision, and it hadn't kept pace with that role. Too much information competed for attention with no clear hierarchy, which made it hard for users to scan the page and trust what they were seeing before paying. The goal was simple to state and harder to execute: restructure and streamline everything on the page so people could actually read it, understand it, and buy with confidence.

Before touching any layout, we built the hypothesis that better information architecture, a stronger visual hierarchy and more readable content would reduce the friction standing between a user and a purchase decision. To test it, we combined interviews and usability tests with recordings of real sessions, instrumenting specific sections of the page so we could measure what people actually interacted with rather than relying only on what they said. That combination confirmed the page didn't have a content problem, it had a structure problem: a large amount of information that was simply organized badly.

The event data told a clear story. Itinerary was by far the most-opened section on the page, which meant it needed to move higher and become easier to scan. Photos mattered more than we expected, with users consistently browsing through several images before deciding anything else. And checking availability turned out to be a meaningful signal on its own: users who checked and didn't buy behaved very differently from users who checked and converted, which changed how prominently that action needed to sit on the page. The redesign condensed the same information into fewer, better-organized sections, prioritizing the data users valued most so the page worked as a quick scan instead of a wall of text.

Why it mattered

The detail page was the last stop before checkout, and a page users couldn't scan quickly was a page they abandoned. Fixing it meant turning interest already earned upstream into completed bookings, without having to change the product itself.

Key decision

Every structural change traced back to a specific behavioral signal instead of intuition: itinerary moved up because it was the most-opened section, photos gained more prominence because of how heavily they were browsed, and the availability check was elevated based on how differently it predicted whether someone would buy.

Outcome

The redesign increased the product page conversion rate by 32%, cut the bounce rate on key sections by 28%, and improved conversion from checking availability to completing a purchase by 25%.

Before: the original detail page packed information into dense blocks with no clear hierarchy, leaving users to scan the whole page just to find the basics.

After: the same information condensed into scannable sections, Overview, then Inclusions & Exclusions side by side, built for a quick scan rather than a full read.

The same condensed structure carried through the rest of the page: departures and returns, cancellation policy and traveller photos, each scannable at a glance.

What scaled

Instrumenting a page section by section before redesigning it, then prioritizing changes by measured behavior instead of assumption, became the standard approach for the rest of the catalogue's product pages.

Redesign of live event maps

Hellotickets' event seat maps were confusing and off-brand, so they were rebuilt around the specific usability problems a heuristic evaluation surfaced.

Client Hellotickets
Responsibility Research · UX Design · Data Driven · Visual Design
Area Seat Selection & Conversion
Partners CEO · CTO

A redesigned seat map turned a confusing, inconsistent booking step into one users could read correctly before they paid.

+33% Increased ticket purchase conversion rate
-60% Decreased support tickets for seat issues
+28% Increased revenue per session

The event seat map was the single interface standing between a user and completing a ticket purchase, and it was quietly costing both conversions and support hours. The goal was to improve clarity and consistency in seat selection while aligning the experience with Hellotickets' own brand identity, rather than leaving it as an off-the-shelf layout bolted onto the rest of the product. The hypothesis was that a more legible, better-structured map would make the booking flow feel native to the site, which alone would lift conversion and cut the friction that was quietly costing revenue.

A heuristic evaluation of the existing booking interface surfaced four recurring problems. On discounted tickets, row selection happened automatically and users had no idea they'd lost the ability to pick their own row. Section labels in the left-hand ticket selector, Mezzanine, Orchestra and so on, didn't match the names shown on the map itself, so users couldn't tell what they were looking at. The maps gave almost no sense of a venue's real structure or the height differences between sections, so people booked without knowing what they'd actually see from their seat. And visually, the maps simply didn't look like the rest of the product, which quietly undermined trust at the exact moment someone was about to pay.

The redesign addressed each of those problems directly. The new maps make a venue's layout and the height differences between sections legible at a glance, with a coloured legend that ties the list of available tickets straight to the matching section on the map, so users can scan and compare without guessing. Once someone selects a section, choosing a specific row happens through a clear dropdown instead of a silent automatic assignment, closing the exact gap that had confused discount buyers.

Why it mattered

A seat map users couldn't read correctly was a seat map they either abandoned or booked the wrong ticket from, and both outcomes showed up directly as lost conversions and avoidable support tickets. Fixing it meant the booking flow finally looked and behaved like it belonged to Hellotickets, at the exact moment trust mattered most.

Key decision

Rather than patching the existing map, the team rebuilt it around the four specific usability problems the heuristic evaluation surfaced: a coloured legend to connect the ticket list to the map, and a clear dropdown to replace the silent automatic row assignment that had confused discount buyers.

Outcome

The redesign increased the ticket purchase conversion rate by 33%, cut support tickets related to seat issues by 60%, and increased revenue per session by 28%.

Before: the heuristic evaluation found no colour legend tying sections to the ticket list, inconsistent section naming, and little sense of the venue's actual layout.

After: a coloured legend ties each section to its matching tickets, and the venue's layout and height differences are now legible at a glance.

What scaled

The coloured legend pattern, tying a list of options directly to their matching location on a visual map, became the template reused across other Hellotickets booking flows wherever users had to match a list to a spatial layout.

Redesign of the search experience

Hellotickets' search rarely matched what people actually typed, so it was rebuilt full-screen around a month of real query data, with recommendations filling the space search alone couldn't.

Client Hellotickets
Responsibility Research · UX Design · Data Driven · Visual Design
Area Search & Conversion
Partners CEO · CTO

A full-screen search built around real query data turned dead ends into recommendations users actually clicked.

-35% Reduced “No Results Found” rate
+28% Improved conversion from recommendations
+50% Boosted recommendations click-through rate

Search is the first real action most users take on Hellotickets, often before they've even settled on what they're looking for, and it wasn't holding up its end of that first interaction. People typed city names, event names, team names, almost anything, and too often got back a sparse results page or nothing at all, which meant the product was losing people at the very first step of their journey, before the rest of the site had a chance to do its job. The goal was to improve the search experience to meet user expectations and increase conversion to the site's most popular activities, not as a cosmetic refresh but as a fix to a conversion bottleneck sitting right at the top of the funnel. The hypothesis was straightforward: a more intuitive, more informative search would align more closely with how people actually searched, cut down on the dead ends they ran into, and let them move toward a confident booking decision faster instead of bouncing off a results page that didn't understand what they'd typed.

Rather than guess at what to change, I instrumented a dedicated event in Google Analytics to study exactly how users interacted with the search engine, what they typed, what matched, what didn't, and where they gave up, then let a full month of real usage accumulate before drawing any conclusions, since a smaller sample risked optimizing for noise instead of an actual pattern. That data surfaced three distinct ways people searched, each one the existing engine handled poorly or not at all. A large share searched by city destination, New York, Miami, and so on, expecting the engine to understand a place name on its own. Nearly as many searched by the specific event they wanted to attend, an NBA game, a Broadway show, rather than browsing a category to find it. And a meaningful share went even more specific, searching for a particular team or niche experience, the New York Yankees, a helicopter tour, which the old engine, built around broader categories, simply wasn't built to match.

Based on that data, the redesign moved to a full-screen search that first gives the user's attention entirely to the search action itself, with no competing navigation or content pulling focus, and then uses the space that frees up to surface recommendations of the site's most searched and best-selling activities and destinations, so even an empty query still gives someone somewhere useful to go. The new engine was rebuilt to handle every pattern the research had surfaced instead of just one: specific venues like Madison Square Garden, cities like New York, live events like the New York Yankees, and the catalogue's own subcategories like New York airport transfers, all matched by the same search instead of forcing users to know in advance which kind of query the engine would understand.

Why it mattered

Search was the entry point to almost every booking on the site, and a search that returned nothing, or returned the wrong kind of result, ended the session before it had really started, with no second chance to recover that user elsewhere in the product. Rebuilding it around real query data instead of internal assumptions meant the fix addressed what people were actually typing, not what the team guessed they might type, which was exactly the gap that had been letting qualified demand slip through unmatched.

Key decision

Rather than redesign search in the abstract or lean on best practices borrowed from elsewhere, the team instrumented real usage first and let a full month of data decide what the engine actually needed to handle: places, cities, live events and subcategories, each one a pattern users were already searching for that the existing engine was missing. Letting evidence set the scope of the redesign, rather than intuition, shaped everything that followed, from the full-screen layout down to which fields the new engine matched against.

Outcome

Once live, the redesign cut the “No results found” rate by 35%, meaning more than a third of the dead ends users used to hit simply stopped happening. Conversion from the new recommendations improved by 28%, and their click-through rate rose by 50%, confirming that giving people something relevant to act on, even when their exact query came up empty, turned a moment of friction into a moment of discovery instead.

The redesigned search surfaces specific activities and cities as you type, and reworks the empty state into a clear, on-brand message instead of a dead end.

What scaled

Instrumenting real search behavior before redesigning became the standard the team applied to other discovery surfaces on the site, and the full-screen search-plus-recommendations pattern was reused wherever a single search box needed to do more work.
