Until recently, Hellotickets' business was built entirely on ticket sales for a large number of events, mainly across Europe and the US. To evolve the product and bring trip experiences into the catalogue for the first time, the company licensed Viator's API, gaining access to its entire product offering and the ability to start expanding the business beyond tickets. Making that integration real meant more than plugging in a feed: it required a major redesign of the website's information architecture to make room for a product type it had never carried before.

The hypothesis was that integrating Viator's API and redesigning the information architecture would significantly increase revenue and user engagement by expanding the catalogue to include trip experiences alongside event ticket sales. Since this was the first time the team was working in the travel experience market, we spent two months on research before touching the design, running 15 interviews with users from around the world to better understand why they chose a particular product or service, build real empathy for their experience, surface their pain points and information gaps, and understand their purchase behavior.

That research surfaced a consistent pattern: the market was crowded and standing out mattered, users carried real doubts about the activities they were considering, and they compared several options before buying, weighing price, free cancellation and language above everything else. They didn't want to register just to make a purchase, they ran the full funnel through to checkout just to see the final price and commissions, and they were willing to pay more on a site that gave them confidence. Those insights shaped a comprehensive redesign of the information architecture built around Viator's catalogue of trip experiences, aimed at a seamless experience that drives both engagement and conversion.

Why it mattered

Trip experiences were a product type Hellotickets had never sold before, and the existing information architecture had nowhere to put them. Without that redesign, the investment in Viator's API would have stayed a backend connection vendors couldn't discover or buy through, never turning into either revenue or engagement.

Key decision

Rather than bolting trip experiences onto the existing, ticket-focused structure, the information architecture was rebuilt around both product types from research, not assumption: two months and 15 interviews across markets shaped which paths and comparison points, price, free cancellation and language, the new structure had to support.

Redesign of the detail page

Hellotickets' trip-experience detail page had grown dense and hard to scan, so the information architecture and visual hierarchy were rebuilt around what research showed users actually needed to decide.

As Hellotickets expanded into trip experiences, the detail page became the single most important page in the purchase decision, and it hadn't kept pace with that role. Too much information competed for attention with no clear hierarchy, which made it hard for users to scan the page and trust what they were seeing before paying. The goal was simple to state and harder to execute: restructure and streamline everything on the page so people could actually read it, understand it, and buy with confidence.

Before touching any layout, we built the hypothesis that better information architecture, a stronger visual hierarchy and more readable content would reduce the friction standing between a user and a purchase decision. To test it, we combined interviews and usability tests with recordings of real sessions, instrumenting specific sections of the page so we could measure what people actually interacted with rather than relying only on what they said. That combination confirmed the page didn't have a content problem, it had a structure problem: a large amount of information that was simply organized badly.

The event data told a clear story. Itinerary was by far the most-opened section on the page, which meant it needed to move higher and become easier to scan. Photos mattered more than we expected, with users consistently browsing through several images before deciding anything else. And checking availability turned out to be a meaningful signal on its own: users who checked and didn't buy behaved very differently from users who checked and converted, which changed how prominently that action needed to sit on the page. The redesign condensed the same information into fewer, better-organized sections, prioritizing the data users valued most so the page worked as a quick scan instead of a wall of text.

Why it mattered

The detail page was the last stop before checkout, and a page users couldn't scan quickly was a page they abandoned. Fixing it meant turning interest already earned upstream into completed bookings, without having to change the product itself.

Key decision

Every structural change traced back to a specific behavioral signal instead of intuition: itinerary moved up because it was the most-opened section, photos gained more prominence because of how heavily they were browsed, and the availability check was elevated based on how differently it predicted whether someone would buy.

Outcome

The redesign increased the product page conversion rate by 32%, cut the bounce rate on key sections by 28%, and improved conversion from checking availability to completing a purchase by 25%.

Before: the original detail page packed information into dense blocks with no clear hierarchy, leaving users to scan the whole page just to find the basics.

After: the same information condensed into scannable sections, Overview, then Inclusions & Exclusions side by side, built for a quick scan rather than a full read.

The redesigned Itinerary section, rebuilt as a stop-by-stop list with photos after session data showed it was the most-opened section on the page.

What scaled

Instrumenting a page section by section before redesigning it, then prioritizing changes by measured behavior instead of assumption, became the standard approach for the rest of the catalogue's product pages.
